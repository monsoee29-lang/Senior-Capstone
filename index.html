🌿 The Problem

Awareness exists. Change doesn't. Why?

Myanmar has no comprehensive national waste management system — yet most plastic ends up burned in open fields or dumped in waterways, both releasing greenhouse gases directly into the atmosphere (Rinasti et al., 2024).

Global plastic production now exceeds 400 million metric tons annually, with single-use plastics (SUPs) representing the most problematic category — designed for one use, disposed of immediately (Simantiri, 2024).

In Myanmar, the problem is compounded by limited formal waste infrastructure, a widespread informal food economy that relies entirely on plastic packaging, and low policy attention to the climate dimensions of plastic disposal (Rinasti et al., 2024).

Most existing research focuses on plastic leakage into waterways, or analyses emissions at a global aggregate level. No prior study had quantified the climate impact of individual plastic consumption behaviors across all regions of Myanmar — until this project (Leal Filho et al., 2025).

The problem of single-use plastic in Myanmar is not one of awareness — it is one of structural embeddedness in everyday life, especially in the informal food economy, where individual choices are severely constrained. — Ei Mon Soe, Capstone Thesis, 2026

💡 Core Insight

The Awareness–Behaviour Gap

Knowing about the problem does not translate to using less plastic. Here's why that matters for policy.

Source: Survey EDA — normalised heatmap across awareness levels. Bars represent relative modal consumption frequency.

One of the study's most significant findings is that environmental awareness does not predict plastic consumption levels. Even "Very Aware" respondents reported using 3–5 or more single-use plastic items per week — the same modal range as less-aware groups.

This is consistent with international literature. Alteneiji et al. (2024) found in the UAE that "awareness campaigns alone are not sufficient without policy interventions." This study confirms the same pattern holds in Myanmar.

A related phenomenon — optimism bias — was also observed: many respondents who self-reported using "much less plastic than others" still counted 3–5 bags per week in their actual usage log. People systematically underestimate their own consumption.

The implication is clear: effective reduction requires supply-side policy targeting markets and vendors — not just individual education campaigns.
🔬 Methodology

How we estimated CO₂ emissions from plastic

A transparent, replicable Python-based approach to converting individual consumption reports into carbon footprint estimates.

No Plastic Item Weight per Unit (g) Emission Factor (kg CO₂e/kg)
1 🛍️ Plastic bags 7 5.4
2 💧 Bottled water 10 5.9
3 🥡 Plastic food containers 20 4.8
4 🥤 Plastic straws 1 4.8
5 🍴 Disposable forks and spoons 3 6.2
6 🧴 Food sachets 2 5.6
Source: Emission factors adapted from Leal Filho, W., Barbir, J., Carpio-Vallejo, E., Dobri, A., & Voronova, V. (2025). Decarbonising the plastic industry: A review of carbon emissions in the lifecycle of plastics production. Science of the Total Environment, 999, 180337. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2025.180337
Core Emission Formula
Estimated CO₂e (kg) = Total plastic used (kg) × Emission factor (kg CO₂e / kg plastic)
1

Item Weights Assigned

Each of 6 plastic item types is assigned a standard weight in grams (e.g. plastic bag = 7g, bottled water = 10g) to convert "number of items" into total mass.

2

Emission Factors Applied

Item-specific lifecycle CO₂e factors drawn from Leal Filho et al. (2025) are applied (e.g. disposable forks: 6.2 kg CO₂e/kg; plastic bags: 5.4 kg CO₂e/kg).

3

Weekly & Annual Totals

Results are aggregated into weekly and annualised CO₂e footprints per respondent, enabling regional and demographic comparisons.

4

Interactive Calculator Built

A public-facing CO₂ Tracker tool lets anyone enter their own plastic habits and see their estimated annual carbon footprint in real time.

Emission estimates are indicative, based on lifecycle averages from peer-reviewed literature. Myanmar-specific disposal-adjusted factors are not yet available.

⚠️ Limitations & Future Work

Honest about what this study can't tell us

Transparency about limitations is part of good data science. Here's what future researchers should address.

📱

Online sampling bias

The survey was distributed digitally, skewing towards young, urban, university-educated respondents. Remote and conflict-affected areas are underrepresented.

🧾

Self-reported data

Respondents may understate plastic usage due to social desirability bias. Future work could combine surveys with waste audit data for validation.

🏭

Global emission factors used

Myanmar-specific lifecycle emission data does not yet exist. CO₂e estimates use global averages from Leal Filho et al. (2025) and should be treated as indicative.

📅

No longitudinal tracking

This is a cross-sectional snapshot. Future research should track changes in consumption behavior over time, particularly in response to policy interventions.
